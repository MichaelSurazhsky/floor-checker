Does your world model beat copying the last frame?

A latent world model predicts the next frame's embedding. Its score, the share of the target's variation it explains (1 is perfect), looks high. But copying the last frame's embedding scores high too, often above 0.9. The question is not how high the score is, but how far above copying.

Guess, then check

A released world model, an environment, and its score. Does it beat copying?

Why copying scores so high

Let ρ be how alike two consecutive frames' embeddings are (their correlation). Copying the last frame then scores 2ρ − 1: the floor. Lift is where a model sits between that floor and a perfect score: lift = (score − floor) / (1 − floor). Lift 0 is no better than copying, 1 is perfect.

Bring your own

Run your model on your own machine, save three arrays of one shape with the feature axis last, and drop them here. Nothing leaves your browser.

import numpy as np
# z_pred: your model's prediction of the embedding of the frame it predicts
# z_next: the encoder's embedding of that frame (the target)
# z_last: the encoder's embedding of the last frame the model was given
np.save("pred.npy",   z_pred.astype(np.float32))
np.save("target.npy", z_next.astype(np.float32))
np.save("last.npy",   z_last.astype(np.float32))

The rows are every axis but the last, so the shape sets the read-out: [frames, D] scores each frame's vector, [frames, patches, D] each patch. A model that predicts k frames ahead copies from k frames back. A few thousand frames are enough to load quickly.

Every released model we measured

One forward pass per row gives the prediction, the copy and the target; the interval is a 95% bootstrap over episodes. Copying, scored as a model, reads lift 0 on every row: the floor is aligned to the frame. The original DINO-WM rows are exploratory: no prediction was sealed before they were measured.
